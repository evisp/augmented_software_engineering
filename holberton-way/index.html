    The Holberton Way¶

    No lectures. Real projects. You own your learning.

    Nobody will stand at a board and teach you here. You build, you get stuck, you get yourself unstuck, and that is the skill. Your peers help you think, and mentors help when you have really tried. This page is how it works, and what it asks of you.

    When you're stuck See the roadmap

    Four ideas to remember¶

    • You own your learning

      The projects, this handbook and the people around you are there to help. The learning itself is your work, every day.

    • Struggle is part of the work

      Being stuck is not a sign that something is wrong. Getting unstuck on your own is exactly what employers pay for.

    • Ask well, at the right time

      Not after two minutes, not after two days. Try for real first, then ask a clear question.

    • Explain what you ship

      If you cannot explain a line, it does not go in. Not from a peer, not from the internet, not from an AI.

    Who does what¶

    Learning here is a team effort, and everyone on the team has a different job.

    • You

      • Work on your projects every day
      • Read the task, the docs and the tutorials
      • Build, test, debug and commit
      • Try before you ask, then ask clearly
      • Explain your code to others

    • Your peers

      • Think through problems with you
      • Pair program and review your code
      • Ask you "why?" until it is clear
      • Learn from your solutions, as you learn from theirs

    • Your mentors

      • Unblock you when you are truly stuck
      • Challenge your approach and your design
      • Give feedback on your work
      • Prepare you for interviews and the job

    Mentors are there for what is hard, not for what is new. A mentor session is most useful when you arrive with a specific problem you have already worked on:

    Mentor sessions are for

    • A blocker you have tried to solve, and can describe
    • A design question: is there a better way to do this?
    • Feedback on code that already works
    • Questions about careers, interviews and how the industry works

    Mentor sessions are not for

    • A topic you have not read about yet
    • A project you have not started
    • Writing your code, or checking every line of it
    • Questions that the task, the docs or a peer can answer in minutes

    How a project goes¶

    Every project on the intranet follows the same path. This handbook covers the second step; the project is where you prove it.

    • Read the task and its learning objectives before you write a line. They tell you what you will be asked to explain.
    • Learn the concepts here, and do the "Try it" exercises and the practice sets. They use different examples from your projects on purpose: the project is yours to solve.
    • Build your solution, and commit small steps as you go.
    • Check it with the Checker, fix, and check again.
    • Review it with peers, and review theirs.
    • Explain it: every line, every choice. That is what a review, a defense and a job interview will ask of you.

    When you're stuck¶

    You will be stuck often. That is normal, and it is where most of the learning happens. What matters is how you get out. Climb the ladder one step at a time, and use your AI assistant alongside every step.

    1. Re-read. The task and the error message, slowly, word by word. Half of all problems are solved right here: a wrong file name, a missed requirement, an error that says exactly what is wrong.
    2. Look it up. The official docs, the man page, the tutorial in this handbook. Search for the exact error message.
    3. Debug. Print the values you think you know. Shrink the problem to the smallest piece of code that still fails. Explain it out loud, to a peer or to a rubber duck: saying it often shows you the mistake.
    4. Ask your peers. In the room or on Slack, with what you tried. Someone has often just solved the same problem, and explaining it helps them too.
    5. Mentor session. When you and your peers are still stuck, bring a prepared question to a mentor.

    Your AI assistant works alongside every step, not only as a last resort: ask it what an error means, what to check next, or to quiz you on a concept. Your general assistant and Clippy, Holberton's assistant on the intranet, are both there for that. Clippy is built to guide you through the reasoning rather than hand you code; use your general assistant the same way.

    How long before you ask?

    Being stuck for twenty minutes is learning. Being stuck for two days is a waste. As a guide: give a problem around twenty minutes of real trying, with steps 1 to 3 and your assistant, before you go to your peers. If you are going in circles, ask sooner.

    Ask a good question¶

    A good question gets a good answer fast, and writing it often solves the problem on its own. Use this template, on Slack or in a session:

    A good question
    What I'm trying to do:  ...
What I expected:        ...
What happened:          ... (the exact error, copied, not retyped)
What I've tried:        ...
The code:               ... (the smallest piece that shows the problem)

    Hard to help

    My SQL doesn't work, can someone help?

    No task, no error, no code. Whoever answers has to ask five questions before they can start.

    Easy to help

    I'm counting loans per member with a LEFT JOIN. I expected Lea to have 0, but she has 1. I tried COUNT(*) and COUNT(member_id). Here is the query: ...

    Anyone can see the problem in a minute, and you have probably spotted it yourself while writing.

    Before a mentor session, check that you can tick all of these:

    • I have climbed steps 1 to 4 of the ladder
    • I can say what I am trying to do, in one sentence
    • I have the exact error, and the smallest code that shows it
    • I can say what I have already tried, and what happened
    • My latest code is pushed, so we can look at it together

    AI, the Holberton way¶

    AI is your amplifier, not your replacement. You will use it every day, and these rules keep it working for you instead of against you:

    • Reason with it

      Ask it to explain errors, concepts and options. Ask it to quiz you. Use it to understand, not to skip understanding.

    • You are responsible

      It drafts; you decide. Whatever you commit is yours, including its mistakes.

    • Predict before you run

      Before you run code from an assistant, say what it should do. If the result surprises you, find out why.

    • Explain every line

      If you cannot explain a line it gave you, ask it to explain, then rewrite it in your own words, or leave it out.

    Habits that work, habits that fail¶

    Habits that work

    • Working a little every day
    • Reading the whole task before starting
    • Committing small steps, with clear messages
    • Asking after real trying, with a prepared question
    • Reading your peers' code, not only your own
    • Explaining your solution out loud before you submit it

    Habits that fail

    • Leaving everything for the weekend before the deadline
    • Starting to code before reading the task
    • One huge push the night before
    • Asking after two minutes, or suffering alone for two days
    • Copying a solution you cannot explain
    • Letting an AI write what you have not understood

    My commitment¶

    Read these, and tick the ones you are ready to commit to. Come back to them when a week goes badly.

    • I work on my projects every day, even when it is only a little
    • I read the task and try for real before I ask for help
    • I ask clear questions, and bring them prepared to mentor sessions
    • I review my peers' code and help them when I can
    • I use AI to understand, not to avoid understanding
    • I can explain every line I push
